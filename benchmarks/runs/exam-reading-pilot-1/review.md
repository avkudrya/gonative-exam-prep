### Секция 1: Saving Seeds for Tomorrow's Harvests

A. For most of the history of agriculture, farmers did not buy their seed; they kept it. After each harvest, the best heads of wheat or the healthiest beans were set aside for the following season, and over thousands of years this quiet selection produced an enormous range of local types, each suited to a particular soil, climate or taste. During the twentieth century, much of that variety began to disappear. As high-yielding modern varieties spread, farmers in many regions abandoned the older types their families had grown for generations, and once a variety is no longer planted, it can vanish within a few seasons. Seed banks were established largely in response to this loss.

B. The value of old varieties is not merely historical. Plant breeders depend on genetic variation, and the traits they need most urgently, such as tolerance of drought, heat or salty soil, or resistance to a newly arrived disease, are often found not in modern crops but in traditional varieties and in the wild species from which crops were domesticated. These wild relatives have survived for millennia without human care, and many carry genes that domestication left behind. Yet they are themselves under threat, since they tend to grow at field edges, on roadsides and in grasslands that are easily ploughed or built on. For this reason, many conservationists regard collecting their seed as no less urgent than preserving farmers' varieties.

C. A seed bank is, at its simplest, a cold store combined with careful record-keeping. When a sample arrives, usually gathered by a collecting team that has noted exactly where and when the plants were found, the seeds are first cleaned of chaff and soil and inspected for damage or insects. They are then dried slowly until only a small fraction of their weight is water. Moisture is the main enemy of a stored seed: if too much remains, ice crystals form when the seeds are frozen and burst their cells. Finally, the seeds are sealed in airtight foil packets or glass jars and placed in rooms kept at around minus eighteen degrees Celsius. Under these conditions, the seeds of many grain crops can remain alive for decades, and some may last for centuries.

D. Storage, however, is not the end of the process. Even in a freezer, seeds slowly age, so staff remove a few seeds from each sample at regular intervals and attempt to germinate them. If the proportion that sprouts falls below an agreed level, the sample must be regenerated: it is planted out, grown to maturity and harvested to produce fresh seed. This step is costly and surprisingly risky. A study led by agronomist Lena Marsh, which examined decades of records from a large national collection, found that regeneration plots of crops such as maize, whose flowers are pollinated by wind, frequently received pollen from neighbouring plants, so the new seed no longer matched the original sample. Marsh concluded that separating plots by distance, although expensive, was essential.

E. Because any single building can be damaged by war, flood or simple mismanagement, collections are commonly duplicated elsewhere. The best-known backup facility is the Svalbard Global Seed Vault, cut into a mountainside on a Norwegian island far inside the Arctic Circle, where the surrounding permafrost would keep the samples cold even if the electricity failed. Deposits there remain the property of the institutions that sent them, and the vault's staff do not open the boxes. The arrangement proved its worth in 2015, when a research centre whose gene bank near Aleppo had been disrupted by the conflict in Syria withdrew copies of its own samples in order to rebuild its working collection elsewhere.

F. Not every plant, however, can be protected in this way. Many species, including cocoa, coconut and numerous tropical trees, produce what botanists call recalcitrant seeds, which die if they are dried and therefore cannot be frozen by the usual method. Other crops, such as bananas and potatoes, are normally grown not from seed but from shoots or tubers, and most cultivated bananas produce no viable seed at all. These must be maintained as living plants in field collections, as tiny plantlets in test tubes, or by cryopreservation, in which small pieces of tissue are stored in liquid nitrogen at far lower temperatures than an ordinary seed bank uses.

G. A deeper criticism concerns what a seed bank cannot preserve. A sample stored in 1980 reflects the conditions of 1980; while it waits in the freezer, the pests, diseases and climate outside continue to change. The ecologist Tomás Ferreira argues that populations growing in farmers' fields and in the wild keep adapting, and that conservation on farms and in protected habitats should be treated as a partner to seed banks rather than an old-fashioned alternative. He adds that a stored seed arrives without the knowledge of how it was grown, cooked or valued, which survives only in the communities that cultivated it. Seed banks, in this view, are an indispensable insurance policy, but not a replacement for living diversity.

### Секция 2: The Hidden Heat of Cities

A. On a still summer evening, a cyclist leaving the centre of a large city for the open fields beyond may notice the air growing noticeably cooler with every kilometre. The effect is not imagined. Meteorologists have long recorded that built-up areas are warmer than the land around them, a pattern known as the urban heat island. The size of the gap varies with the city and the weather, but it is often a few degrees and can, on calm, clear nights, exceed ten. Surprisingly, the contrast is usually greatest after dark rather than in the heat of the afternoon. During the day, sunshine warms town and countryside alike; it is at night, when fields cool quickly but streets go on releasing the warmth they have stored, that the difference becomes most obvious.

B. Several features of the built environment combine to produce this effect. The first is the choice of materials. Asphalt, brick and concrete absorb a large share of the sunlight that falls on them and hold that energy for hours, behaving much like the stones of a storage heater. The second is the absence of plants. In the countryside, a good deal of the sun's energy is spent evaporating water from soil and leaves, a process that cools the surroundings in the same way that sweat cools the skin; in a city where rain runs quickly into drains, far less energy is used up in this way. The shape of the city matters too. Tall buildings lining narrow streets form what climate scientists call 'urban canyons', which block cooling breezes and prevent heat from escaping to the open sky at night. Finally, cities generate heat of their own, from engines, factories and, ironically, the air-conditioning units that push warm air out of buildings and into the street.

C. The consequences go well beyond discomfort. During heatwaves, death rates rise sharply in cities, and doctors point out that the warm nights may be as dangerous as the hot days, because the body never gets the chance to recover. Elderly people living alone, and those with heart or lung conditions, are at particular risk. Higher temperatures also raise the demand for electricity as residents switch on fans and air conditioners, which in turn can strain power networks at exactly the moment they are most needed. Where that electricity comes from burning fossil fuels, the extra demand adds to the emissions that are warming the planet as a whole. Heat even affects the air people breathe: warm, sunny conditions speed up the chemical reactions that form ground-level ozone, a pollutant that irritates the lungs.

D. Nor is the burden shared equally. When a team led by the geographer Marta Venn mapped surface temperatures across one large port city using satellite images, they found differences of up to seven degrees between neighbourhoods only a few kilometres apart. The hottest districts were, almost without exception, the poorest, with densely packed housing, few parks and wide roads offering little shade. Wealthier suburbs, by contrast, were lined with mature trees and private gardens. Venn argues that this pattern is no accident, since decisions about where to plant trees, build highways or locate industry have often been made with little regard for the people living nearby. Residents of the hottest areas are also the least likely to own air conditioning or to be able to afford running it.

E. Cities have begun to fight back, and the simplest measures involve changing the colour of surfaces. Roofs coated with pale, reflective paint can stay many degrees cooler than dark ones on a sunny day, lowering the temperature of the rooms beneath them as well as of the surrounding air. Some authorities are experimenting with lighter-coloured paving for the same reason. Vegetation offers a second approach. Trees shade streets and walls while also releasing water vapour through their leaves, and parks can form pockets of cooler air that spread into neighbouring blocks. In a three-year trial, the urban ecologist Tomas Reiner compared streets that had received new tree planting with similar streets that had not, and recorded afternoon air temperatures up to two degrees lower beneath the young canopy. Green roofs, covered with low-growing plants, also help, though Reiner found their effect at street level to be modest, since most of the cooling stays on the rooftop.

F. None of these solutions is perfect. Reflective pavements can bounce sunlight back onto pedestrians and the walls of nearby buildings, so that people walking on them may actually feel hotter. Trees need water, space for their roots and years of care before they provide meaningful shade, and in dry regions irrigating them may compete with other needs. For these reasons, most specialists now favour a mixture of measures chosen to suit local conditions rather than a single remedy. The heat island will not disappear while cities remain dense collections of stone and steel, but with careful planning its worst effects can be softened. As global temperatures continue to rise, that task is likely to become one of the most pressing challenges facing those who design and run the world's cities.

### Секция 3: Playing to Learn?

A. In many countries, the first years of school have become a battleground. On one side are those who believe that young children learn best through play: building, pretending, sorting and exploring. On the other are those who argue that the early years are precious precisely because they are the time to master the basics of reading and arithmetic, and that every hour spent in the sand tray is an hour taken from the alphabet. The argument is usually framed as a contest between two camps, and in my view that framing has done more damage than anything either side has actually said. The evidence, read carefully, points to a less dramatic but more useful conclusion: play can be a powerful way of teaching, but only when adults shape it with clear purposes in mind.

B. The case for play has been made most forcefully by Dr Helena Varga, a developmental psychologist whose team followed some 300 five-year-olds through their first school year. Half of the classes used what Varga calls 'guided play': activities that children experience as games but that teachers have designed around a specific goal, such as a pretend shop in which prices must be compared, or a treasure hunt that requires reading simple clues. The other classes received conventional whole-class lessons covering the same content. By the end of the year, children in the guided-play classes knew more mathematical vocabulary and could explain their reasoning more fully. Varga argues that the explanation lies in motivation: a child who wants to win the shop game has a reason to care whether seven is more than five. I find her results persuasive, though it is worth noting that her classes were small and her teachers volunteered for the project, which may say as much about their enthusiasm as about the method.

C. Not everyone is convinced. Professor Tomas Reinholt, who studies the economics of education, points out that early advantages of many kinds tend to disappear. In his own review of comparable programmes, children who had experienced play-heavy classrooms were ahead at six but indistinguishable from their peers by the age of eight. For Reinholt, this 'fade-out' suggests that schools are investing time in a method that produces only a temporary lead. He is especially critical of attempts to teach reading through play alone, insisting that the link between letters and sounds must be taught directly and systematically, since few children discover it by themselves. On this last point I think he is right, and many supporters of play now accept it. But his wider conclusion does not follow. A lead that vanishes from test scores may reappear elsewhere, and tests designed for eight-year-olds measure a fairly narrow set of skills.

D. That is the argument of Dr Lucien Barre, who has spent a decade measuring what he calls 'self-regulation': a child's ability to wait, to follow rules, to plan and to recover from frustration. In Barre's studies, children who regularly took part in structured pretend play, in which they had to stay in role and follow agreed rules, showed stronger self-control a year later than those who did not. He believes these capacities, rather than early reading scores, are what predict success later in school. Barre is careful, however, not to claim that play improves academic results directly; his position is that researchers have been looking in the wrong place for its benefits.

E. Another researcher complicates the picture further. Dr Amara Osei spent two years observing classrooms that described themselves as play-based, and she found that the label meant very different things. In some, teachers moved among the children, asking questions and introducing new words. In others, 'play time' was essentially unsupervised free time, during which teachers caught up on paperwork. Osei argues that much of the disagreement between Varga and Reinholt disappears once this is recognised: studies that found no lasting benefit may simply have been studying classrooms where little teaching took place. She is sceptical of any research, on either side, that does not record what adults were actually doing.

F. Why, then, has play lost ground in so many early classrooms? Part of the answer is pressure from national testing, which rewards what can be quickly counted. Part is that guided play is harder to do well than either free play or traditional lessons. Ingrid Solberg, who trains early-years teachers, reports that many new teachers have never seen skilled guided play in action, and that they fall back on worksheets because worksheets are easy to plan and easy to check. She proposes that trainee teachers spend time in classrooms where the approach is practised well, rather than simply reading about it.

G. It is tempting to declare a winner, but the research does not allow it. The strongest version of the anti-play argument, that play is a pleasant distraction from learning, is not supported by the evidence. Nor is the strongest version of the pro-play argument, that children left to themselves will discover everything they need. What the studies suggest is that the adult matters more than the activity. A well-designed game led by an attentive teacher can teach mathematical language and self-control at the same time; a pile of blocks in a corner, ignored by everyone, teaches very little. Schools that want the benefits of play should therefore invest less in equipment and more in the people who use it.

The result is a frozen archive, stacked in order from the most recent snowfall at the surface to the oldest ice near the rock beneath, and reading it has become one of the most powerful ways of discovering what the Earth's climate was like long before anyone kept weather records.

B. Retrieving the archive begins with choosing where to drill. According to glaciologist Mirela Kossak, who has led several expeditions for the Polar Stratigraphy Group, the ideal site is a broad dome where the ice is thick but moves slowly. "Near the coast, ice flows towards the sea and its layers get stretched and tangled," she explains. "On a dome, they stay roughly where they were laid down." The drill itself is a hollow rotating tube with cutting teeth at its lower end. Lowered on a cable, it carves out a cylinder of ice a few metres long, which is hauled to the surface before the drill descends again. Because the enormous pressure at depth would gradually squeeze the borehole shut, the hole is filled with a fluid whose density roughly matches that of the surrounding ice. Reaching the bottom of a sheet three kilometres thick can take several summer seasons.

C. Not every core can travel straight away. At depths of a few hundred metres, the trapped air is under such high pressure that the ice tends to crack or shatter when it is brought up. Kossak's team found that this brittle ice could be handled safely only if it was left to rest in a cold store at the drilling site for about a year, allowing the stresses inside it to relax. Eventually, each section is measured, photographed and labelled with its depth before being packed into insulated containers and shipped to a laboratory, where it is kept well below freezing, since even brief warming could alter its chemistry.

D. Before any measurement means anything, the ice has to be dated. In the upper part of a core, this can be done by counting annual layers, much as one might count the rings of a tree. Summer and winter snow differ in texture and in the amount of dust and sea salt they contain, so each year usually leaves a recognisable band. Deeper down, however, the weight above has thinned the layers until they are too narrow to tell apart. Here researchers turn to other clues. Thin bands of ash from large volcanic eruptions act as time markers that can be matched between cores and sometimes linked to eruptions of known date. Beyond that, computer models of how the ice has flowed and compressed are used to estimate the ages of the deepest sections.

E. With a timescale in place, the analysis can begin. The most widely used temperature signal comes from the water itself. Oxygen atoms exist in lighter and heavier forms, and the colder the air when snow forms, the smaller the proportion of heavy oxygen it contains. By measuring this ratio along a core, scientists can trace past warming and cooling. The bubbles offer something more direct: genuine samples of ancient atmosphere, in which concentrations of greenhouse gases such as carbon dioxide and methane can be measured. Dust provides a third line of evidence, since ice laid down during glacial periods typically contains far more of it, reflecting a drier, windier world.

F. Impressive as these records are, they have limits. One problem is that the air in a bubble is always younger than the ice around it. Near the surface, snow remains porous for decades or longer, and air keeps circulating through it until the pores finally close. At sites with very little snowfall, the gap between the age of the ice and that of its air can reach several thousand years, and it must be estimated rather than measured. The bottom of the sheet brings further difficulty: heat rising from the rock can melt the deepest ice, and layers close to the bed are often folded, so the oldest part of the record may be scrambled or missing. Finally, a core describes conditions at a single location. Kossak warns that a temperature history from central Antarctica should not be treated as a global average, and that ice records are most valuable when compared with evidence from ocean sediments and caves.

G. At present, the longest continuous ice-core record reaches back about 800,000 years. Many researchers hope to go further, to a period roughly a million years ago when the rhythm of the ice ages appears to have changed. Ice that old will only be found where the bed is cold and the ice has barely moved, and the Polar Stratigraphy Group has spent three seasons surveying possible sites with radar. Kossak is cautiously optimistic: such ice probably exists, she says, but it may form a layer only a few metres thick at the very bottom of the sheet.

### Секция 3: In Defence of Boredom

A. Few states of mind have a worse reputation than boredom. We speak of being "bored to death", we apologise to guests if an evening drags, and we treat a dull afternoon as time that has simply been lost. Modern life has organised itself around making sure that such afternoons never happen: an entire entertainment industry now competes to fill every spare minute, and the smartphone means that nobody need endure even a few seconds of empty time in a queue or on a station platform. The assumption behind all this is that boredom is a defect, something to be cured as quickly as possible. I believe this assumption is wrong. A moderate amount of boredom is not merely bearable; it is one of the more useful experiences an ordinary day can offer.

B. To see why, it helps to consider what boredom actually is. Psychologists generally describe it as an uncomfortable state in which we have attention to spare but can find nothing that seems worth spending it on. Understood in this way, boredom is less like an illness and more like hunger. Hunger is unpleasant, yet nobody regards it as a fault in the body; it is a message that we need to eat. Boredom, similarly, tells us that what we are currently doing is not rewarding enough and that we should change it. It is a signal, not a malfunction. Reaching for a distraction the moment it appears is rather like swallowing a painkiller to cope with a stone in your shoe: the discomfort fades, but the cause remains exactly where it was.

C. The habit of silencing this signal may also be changing our attention itself. The attention researcher Marta Lindqvist followed two groups of commuters for six weeks. One group used their phones throughout every journey; the other agreed to travel without them. At the end of the period, both groups were given the same unexciting reading task, and those who had spent their journeys on their phones rated it as more tedious and abandoned it considerably earlier. Lindqvist's interpretation is that constant stimulation raises the threshold at which ordinary tasks begin to feel tedious, so that activities which once seemed acceptable come to feel intolerable. Her study was small, and I would not claim that it proves phones damage concentration on their own; people who choose to look at screens all day may differ in other ways. But it fits an experience many of us will recognise: the more entertainment we consume, the less patience we seem to have for anything that is not entertaining.

D. Boredom's best-known defenders point to creativity, and here there is genuine evidence, though less than enthusiasts suggest. In one experiment by the cognitive psychologist Tomas Ferreira, volunteers spent twenty minutes sorting a large box of buttons by colour, while a second group watched a short comedy clip. Both groups were then given the opening of a story and asked to write as many different endings as they could. The button-sorters produced a noticeably wider range of endings. Ferreira believes that when the outside world offers too little, the mind begins to wander in search of the stimulation it lacks, and this wandering brings unrelated ideas together. It is an attractive finding, but popular writing has often turned it into a promise that boredom will make anyone inventive. It will not. Boredom creates room for new ideas; it does not supply them.

E. Nor should every kind of boredom be welcomed. Chronic boredom, the kind experienced by people confined for long periods or trapped in work of unbroken monotony, is associated with low mood, poor health and risky behaviour. I have no wish to defend it. The distinction that matters is between a passing episode, which we are free to respond to, and a lasting condition from which there is no way out. The value of brief boredom lies precisely in that response: it pushes us to look for something better to do, to think, or to notice what is around us. When escape is impossible, the signal becomes nothing more than suffering.

F. This is why I think the modern instinct to rescue children from boredom deserves more scrutiny. Many parents feel that a child complaining of having nothing to do represents a failure on their part, and they hurry to provide an activity or a screen. Yet children who are left to deal with empty time themselves often learn to invent their own occupations, constructing games, stories and projects that no adult would have thought to suggest. In removing the discomfort, we may also remove the opportunity to discover how to make use of it.

G. None of this means that we should seek out tedium or abandon entertainment. The practical lesson is modest. Leave the phone in your pocket during short waits; let a slow train journey be slow; notice the restless feeling of boredom and ask what it is telling you before deciding what to do about it. The goal is not to enjoy being bored, but to stop running from it, and to treat it as information rather than as an enemy.
