### Секция 3: Riders Against the Clock

A. Anyone who has stood at a busy junction in central London, New York or Chicago has probably seen one: a rider with a bag slung across the back, weaving between taxis and buses with a speed that looks almost reckless. The bicycle courier seems like a product of the modern, impatient city, yet the job is considerably older than most people imagine. Its history, with its sudden booms and near-disappearances, offers a surprisingly clear picture of how city dwellers have exchanged information, and how often they have changed their minds about the best way to do it.

B. The story begins in the late nineteenth century, when improved bicycle designs made cycling practical for ordinary people. Telegraph companies quickly saw the advantage. A telegram could cross a continent in minutes, but it still had to be carried from the local office to the person whose name was on the envelope, and that final stretch was often the slowest part of the journey. Offices had long employed boys to run these messages on foot; given bicycles, the same boys could reach far more addresses in a single shift. Most were teenagers, and many were paid by the message rather than by the hour, which encouraged them to ride fast. According to urban historian Beatrix Oyelaran, this arrangement 'turned a scattered group of errand boys into the first real delivery network of the industrial city.' She regards it as the model for every courier service that followed.

C. During the twentieth century, the trade gradually lost ground. As telephones spread into offices and homes, fewer messages needed to be written down and carried at all, and motor vans took over most of the heavier deliveries, so in many cities the cycling messenger became a far less familiar sight. The revival came in the 1970s and 1980s, and it was driven by traffic. Financial and legal districts in large cities had become so congested that a car could take longer to cross a few blocks than a person on foot. At the same time, banks, law firms and advertising agencies depended on original paper documents, often ones that needed a signature, arriving the same day. Courier companies responded by hiring riders, who could slip through stationary traffic and deliver a contract across the city in under twenty minutes. Riders usually received a share of the fee for each job, so the busiest could earn a good living.

D. With the revival came a distinctive culture. Riders communicated with a central dispatcher by two-way radio, developed their own slang and often favoured simple, tough machines, including fixed-gear bikes with few parts to break. Informal races between couriers grew into organised events, some of them international. Yusuf Demirel, who rode in central London for eleven years, remembers the work as 'half sport, half survival'. The survival part was real. Most riders were self-employed, so they had no sick pay, and a single collision could leave them without income for weeks. Demirel recalls that experienced couriers would teach newcomers which streets to avoid at certain times of day, a kind of knowledge that, as he puts it, 'no map could give you.'

E. From the late 1980s, the industry faced its most serious threat yet. Fax machines became standard office equipment and email soon followed, allowing documents to travel between offices in seconds, and demand for paper deliveries fell sharply. Many courier firms closed, but others adapted by changing what they carried. Things that cannot be sent down a cable, such as medical samples, computer parts and items needed urgently on film sets, became an increasingly important part of the business. A study by transport researcher Camille Thornquist, who examined the job records of one mid-sized courier firm, found that documents made up roughly four in every five deliveries in 1990 but fewer than one in three by 2005. Her conclusion is that the firms which survived were those that stopped thinking of themselves as paper carriers.

F. Today, the rider with a bag on the back is more likely to be delivering a takeaway meal than a legal contract. Smartphone apps now assign jobs automatically, replacing the human dispatcher, and many riders use electric bikes that reduce the physical strain of long shifts. Some veterans of the earlier era feel that the sense of community has weakened, since app riders rarely meet each other. Food delivery riders also face many of the same dangers as their predecessors, and in several countries there has been heated debate over whether they should count as employees. Yet the bicycle's basic advantages have not changed. Several cities are now encouraging cargo bikes for the last stage of parcel deliveries, hoping to reduce both congestion and air pollution. More than a century after the first telegraph boys pedalled away from their offices, the courier's essential skill remains the same: getting something small across a crowded city faster than anything else can.
